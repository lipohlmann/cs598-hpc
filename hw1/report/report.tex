\subsection{$N_{1/2}$}

$N_{1/2}$ -- the smallest matrix size at which a curve reaches half of its own peak GFLOPS -- differs sharply between the two:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{C (this work):} peak 33.149 GFLOPS/core; half-peak is 16.575 GFLOPS, first reached at $N_{1/2} = 72$.
    \item \textbf{MatLab:} peak 80.151 GFLOPS/core; half-peak is 40.076 GFLOPS, first reached at $N_{1/2} = 488$.
\end{itemize}
The serial C kernel has essentially no dispatch overhead, so it is already near its (lower) asymptotic rate at very small $N$. MatLab's curve in \cref{fig:comparison} is visibly a step function: below roughly $N=700$ it tracks the C curve or falls under it, then jumps abruptly once $N$ is large enough that MKL's internal multithreading pays for itself, climbing toward its 8-CPU peak. That threading start-up cost is exactly why MatLab needs a much larger $N$ to reach half of its own peak, even though its peak is higher.

\subsection{Comparison to Iteration 1}

Iteration 1 (the naive triple loop, \cref{fig:naive}) peaked at only 0.378 GFLOPS/core (at $N=40$) and settled to a floor of about 0.199 GFLOPS/core for large $N$, since its innermost loop strides through $B$ with stride \texttt{B\_cols} instead of reading it contiguously. Against that baseline, the final version is:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $33.149 / 0.378 \approx 88\times$ faster at each curve's own peak.
    \item $27.372 / 0.199 \approx 138\times$ faster at $N=1992$, comparing large-matrix performance directly.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Process and Lessons Learned}

The largest single-step gains, in order, came from: (1) loop reordering plus a register-blocked microkernel to stop re-walking $A$ and $B$ from scratch for every output entry, (2) blocking the $K$ and $M$ loops so a slab of $B$ -- already contiguous in row-major storage -- stays cache-resident across many row-blocks of $A$ instead of being re-streamed from memory each time, and (3) matching the microkernel's vector width to what the compute node's CPU actually supports.

Step (2) is a cautionary tale about environment mismatch: it produced a large, clean improvement on a local development machine (eliminating a severe falloff at large $N$), but made \emph{no measurable difference at all} on the actual cluster node -- the peak GFLOPS was identical before and after, and unaffected by changing \texttt{--cpus-per-task} from 1 to 8 and back. That flat ceiling was the signal that the bottleneck on the real hardware wasn't memory bandwidth at all. Rather than keep guessing, we added a short diagnostic block to \texttt{batch\_q1.sh} to print the compute node's CPU model, advertised vector ISA, and what \texttt{-march=native} actually resolves to -- which is what surfaced step (3): the node is an Ice Lake Xeon with full AVX-512, but the Makefile was hard-capping vectorization at 256 bits and the microkernel tile was only sized for one 256-bit register per row, together leaving roughly half of the chip's per-core throughput unused. That single fix accounted for most of the total improvement. The broader lesson: a local machine is not a reliable proxy for the target HPC hardware, and it's worth pulling concrete diagnostics from the actual compute node early rather than assuming the same bottleneck applies everywhere.

We are satisfied with the final result. A peak of 33.149 GFLOPS/core against MatLab's 80.151 is a real gap, but part of that gap is not apples-to-apples: MatLab's ``per-core'' figure is an 8-CPU MKL run divided by 8, not a directly measured single-core run, and the plateau in \cref{fig:comparison} strongly suggests it depends on multithreading to reach its peak at all. Closing more of the remaining gap on the C side would mean packing $A$/$B$ into aligned contiguous panels and retuning the $K/M$ block sizes for the Ice Lake node's actual cache sizes rather than the development machine's, but the current result is a reasonable, well-understood stopping point for a serial kernel.
